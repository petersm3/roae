The narrative document's audience is a reader who is not going to run anything. For that reader a
figure is not decoration; it is the only load-bearing evidence they will actually inspect. Two are
needed, and they are different in kind.

## Figure N-1 — the object (narrative §1)

**What it must show:** what a King Wen ordering *is*, before any claim is made about it. 64
hexagrams, 32 pairs, the pairing rule visible, and the sequence running through them.

**Why §1 specifically:** every later claim is about a property of this object. A reader who reaches
§4 without a mental picture of the object is reading assertions about a thing they cannot see.

**Constraint:** it must be drawn from the actual King Wen sequence, not a schematic. The pairing is
the content; a stylised diagram that gets the pairing approximately right is worse than no figure,
because it looks authoritative.

## Figure N-2 — the f·g mechanism (narrative §§4–5)

**What it must show:** how the forward and backward ladders meet — that a count at layer `k` is a
product of what arrives from one end and what departs from the other, and that this is why an exact
count is possible without enumeration.

**Why it is the hard one:** §§4–5 are where the document asks the reader to accept that a number was
computed rather than counted. That is the least intuitive claim in the whole arc, and it is currently
carried entirely by prose.

**Constraint:** the figure must not imply the ladders are *sampled*. The mechanism is exact; a
drawing that suggests estimation would undercut the very claim it is illustrating.

## What is NOT decided here

Rendering, palette, and whether N-2 is one image or a two-panel sequence. Those are drafting choices.
What this page fixes is that both figures are **planned rows with stated jobs and stated failure
modes**, so the drafter inherits a specification rather than a blank space.
